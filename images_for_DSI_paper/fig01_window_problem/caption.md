**Figure 1.** The estimation-window problem, illustrated with synthetic data. All series and curves are illustrative; they are generated from fixed formulas and do not come from the Gulf of Thailand data. (A) Three candidate estimation windows, each ending in the final year (top), drawn over a synthetic commercial catch-per-unit-effort series (CPUE, middle) and fishing effort (bottom). The dashed lines mark two structural breaks, after years 20 and 40, which separate three periods: early (grey), when effort builds up and CPUE declines steeply with effort; middle (light blue), when CPUE drops to a low, flat level while effort stays high; and recent (dark blue), when effort is cut sharply and CPUE partly recovers. Each window bar has the colour of the period it adds: the full history spans all three periods, the post-break window the middle and recent periods, and the recent window only the last. Points in A and B use the same colours. (B) What each window gives the model: log(CPUE) against effort, with the least-squares line and its 95% confidence band. Each period has its own CPUE–effort relationship. The full-history slope averages three relationships and is driven mainly by the offsets between periods; the post-break slope mixes two; the recent window reflects a single relationship but rests on only ten years. A window that mixes periods can still show high contrast and a strong apparent depletion signal, which DSI rewards. (C) The conceptual trade-off as the first year of the window moves later (every window ends in the final year). The informative curve (blue, solid) is the information a window gives the model: longer windows hold more years and more contrast in effort and CPUE, which is what allows a production model to estimate its parameters, so the curve is highest for a window starting in year 1 and falls as the start moves later, steeply for the shortest windows. The representative curve (grey, dashed) is how well the window reflects the current system; it steps up after each break, because each later start removes a period that no longer applies. Their product, suitability (black, thick), jumps at each break and declines between them. The green diamond marks the peak of suitability, the start year that best balances information against representativeness, and uses the same symbol as the DSIr optimum in Fig. 3. In this example it falls at the first year after break 1, where the gain in information outweighs the inclusion of the middle period; the recent window is fully representative but too short to score highest. Triangles mark the starts of the three windows in A. Each curve is scaled to its own maximum, so only the shapes and the position of the peak carry meaning. DSI and DSIr screen the information side of this trade-off and the breakpoint analysis screens the representativeness side; the candidate windows combine both (Methods). In real data the breaks are neither known in advance nor necessarily sharp, which is why the windows are compared with the model-based diagnostics in Figs 5 and 6.
